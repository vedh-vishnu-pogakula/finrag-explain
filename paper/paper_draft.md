## Abstract

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems for financial question answering are routinely
evaluated with automatic *faithfulness* metrics, and the field's current guidance — from
RAGBench, ARES, and the RAGAS library's own `FaithfulnesswithHHEM` variant — is to replace the
metric's LLM judge with a cheaper fine-tuned verifier. We test whether the metric survives that
substitution on numerical financial QA. On 250 questions each from FinQA and TAT-QA, answered by
a fixed open-weights generator (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, 4-bit, program-of-thought), we score the
*same* answers and contexts with three verifiers — an NLI cross-encoder, a 3.8B LLM, and a 7B
LLM — and audit each on four independent axes: separation of correct from incorrect answers,
specificity of the score to targeted evidence removal, agreement with a deterministic
operand-provenance reference, and statement-level agreement between verifiers. The cheap
verifier fails the audit: on FinQA it scores *wrong* answers as more faithful than correct ones
(separation −0.098), its verdicts agree with the LLM verifier's at chance (Cohen's κ = −0.147),
and its score collapses to whether the answer *restates* a figure present in the evidence
(0.476 vs 0.195, p < 0.001) — a consequence of entailment models being unable to verify
arithmetic. The 7B LLM verifier passes every axis (separation +0.251, specificity +0.305, both
p < 0.001), while the 3.8B LLM reports the highest mean faithfulness of any verifier and
separates on neither dataset — so verifier *capacity*, not verifier *type*, decides validity. We
further show that passing a perturbation audit is necessary but not sufficient for metric
validity: the cheap verifier is evidence-specific (+0.070 ± 0.013 over four seeds) precisely
because the operand-supplying sentence is also the lexically overlapping one. Two artefacts
follow: a labeled dataset of 1,500 (question, verifier) verdicts with mechanical failure labels,
and a training-free retrieval-attribution layer anchored on RankingSHAP whose Shapley estimator
lifts comprehensiveness over a random baseline by 0.451 versus occlusion's 0.314. Composed into
a full system, a deterministic-provenance-plus-LLM-verifier gate raises the accuracy of answers
a user is told to trust from 0.45 to 0.72 on FinQA and 0.48 to 0.64 on TAT-QA; the cheap
verifier's gate alone (0.38) is worse than trusting everything. The entire study runs at zero
monetary cost on open weights and a free Colab T4, and all code, checkpoints and result files
are released.

## 1 Introduction

Financial question answering over filings and earnings reports is a demanding target for
retrieval-augmented generation: most answers are numbers, most numbers are *computed* from
several figures in the evidence rather than copied from it, and a wrong number is costly.
Practitioners therefore attach automatic faithfulness metrics to their RAG pipelines, most
commonly the RAGAS *faithfulness* score, which decomposes an answer into atomic statements and
asks a judge whether each is supported by the retrieved context.

The judge is the expensive part. RAGAS's default judge is an LLM; the library ships a second
variant, `FaithfulnesswithHHEM`, that swaps the LLM for Vectara's fine-tuned HHEM model, and
the RAGBench and ARES benchmarks both argue for the same substitution: fine-tuned or NLI-style
verifiers are cheaper, faster, and — on general-domain data — about as good. This paper asks a
narrow question that this guidance leaves open: **on numerical financial QA, does the metric
still measure faithfulness after the substitution?**

We answer it by *auditing the auditor*. Holding the generator, retriever, questions and
retrieved contexts fixed, we score the identical answers with three verifiers and test each
on four independent axes (§5). None of the axes requires a human label of faithfulness: two
use the datasets' gold answers, one uses a deterministic reference we derive from the
generator's own arithmetic, and one uses inter-verifier agreement. The result is not that RAGAS
is broken — its default LLM judge at 7B passes every axis — but that the cheap substitute
inverts on this domain, and that a cheaper *LLM* inverts too. The mechanism is measurable and
simple: entailment models cannot perform arithmetic, so faithfulness collapses to lexical
restatement.

**Contributions.**

1. **A four-axis validity audit for faithfulness verifiers** (separation, perturbation
   specificity, deterministic provenance, inter-verifier agreement) and a *domain-specific
   counterexample* to the field's substitution guidance, with the mechanism identified
   (§5, §7.3). We show that perturbation sensitivity is necessary but not sufficient for
   validity: a verifier can respond to the right evidence for the wrong reason (§7.3.2).
2. **A labeled failure-case dataset**: 1,500 (question, verifier) verdicts across two datasets
   and three verifiers, each carrying mechanical labels — false negative, false positive,
   insensitive, nonspecific, unscorable — derived from files, not from annotators, and therefore
   reproducible to the row (§5.4, §7.3.5).
3. **Training-free retrieval attribution** for financial RAG, anchored on RankingSHAP and
   Rank-LIME rather than a naive SHAP/LIME call, evaluated as faithfulness against a random-unit
   baseline (§4, §7.2).
4. **A composed system and a user-facing number**: a verification gate built from
   deterministic operand provenance and the validated verifier raises the accuracy of trusted
   answers from 0.45 to 0.72 on FinQA (§7.4).

Everything runs on open weights at zero monetary cost (a free Colab T4 for generation, a laptop
for everything else), with pinned dependencies and per-question checkpoints, so every number
can be regenerated.

## 2 Related Work

**Financial QA.** FinQA (Chen et al., 2021) pairs S&P 500 earnings-report pages with questions
whose gold answers are executable programs over figures in the page; TAT-QA (Zhu et al., 2021)
mixes tables and text with arithmetic, span and multi-span answers. FinQA's program-based
formulation motivates our program-of-thought generation (Chen et al., 2023): the model emits an
arithmetic expression that is executed by a sandboxed evaluator, and FinQA's official
execution-accuracy metric scores against the executed gold program (`exe_ans`).

**Financial RAG.** The closest architectural precedent is Bayesian RAG (Ngartera et al.
[verify]), which adds MC-Dropout uncertainty penalties to retrieval scoring over SEC filings
but provides neither a source-attribution trail nor any test of its faithfulness metric; this
work addresses those two gaps and does not reimplement its architecture. FinRAG-12B
(Katerenchuk et al., 2026 [verify]) is a production banking RAG with document-level citation
tags and a static faithfulness sub-score; it is proprietary and we cite it as the deployed
counterpart of the setting we study rather than as a baseline.

**Faithfulness metrics for RAG.** RAGAS (Es et al., 2024) computes faithfulness as the fraction
of answer statements a judge deems supported by the context. ARES (Saad-Falcon et al., 2024)
and RAGBench (Friel et al., 2024) both advocate replacing LLM judges with fine-tuned lightweight
judges, and RAGAS ships `FaithfulnesswithHHEM` using Vectara's HHEM (Hughes & Bae, 2023
[verify]). The model-based verifier family — FactCC (Kryscinski et al., 2020), SummaC (Laban
et al., 2022), QAFactEval (Fabbri et al., 2022), AlignScore (Zha et al., 2023) — was developed
for summarisation, where support is textual; none was designed to verify that a number was
correctly *computed* from others, which is the gap our results expose.

**Explaining retrieval.** Feature attribution for ranking differs from attribution for
classification because the object being explained is an ordering. RankingSHAP (Heuss et al.,
2024 [verify]) defines a listwise Shapley value over a rank-based value function; Rank-LIME
(Chowdhury et al., 2023 [verify]) fits a local linear surrogate to the ranking. We instantiate
both, plus occlusion (Zeiler & Fergus, 2014), on the dense retriever's similarity function,
and evaluate them as *faithfulness* explanations (comprehensiveness/sufficiency against a
random baseline) because no gold explanation exists.

## 3 System

### 3.1 Retrieval and generation (baseline B1)

Documents are split into chunks by a tokenizer-enforced budget of 256 tokens of the embedding
model; FinQA tables are linearised one row per chunk with header→value pairs made explicit,
and TAT-QA's multi-row headers are resolved by the loader. Chunks are embedded with
`BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5` (Xiao et al., 2023) and searched with exact inner product within the
question's own document (the scope FinQA's `gold_inds` and TAT-QA's `rel_paragraphs` were
labelled for), returning the top five.

The generator is `Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct` in 4-bit NF4 with double quantisation (Dettmers et al.,
2023) and greedy decoding, run on a free Colab T4. The prompt binds the model to the retrieved
evidence, asks for a JSON object with an answer, an `answer_expression`, cited evidence
numbers, and an `insufficient_evidence` flag, and includes three fixed worked examples in the
system message. The expression is executed by a whitelisted-AST arithmetic evaluator (never
`eval`), and only when it actually computes something (`"4.35%"` is not executed). This
configuration — model, quantisation, program-of-thought, exemplar count — is held fixed across
every baseline in the paper: changing any one would invalidate B1/B2/B3 comparability.

### 3.2 Evidence grounding (supporting layer)

A grounding layer maps each answer claim to the sentence or table row that supports it. For a
numeric answer, the claims are the *operands* of `answer_expression` (scaffold constants such
as 100 excluded), and each operand is looked up by exact magnitude in the segmented evidence.
This is deterministic and involves no model; it yields the exact `(chunk, sentence)` that
supplied each figure, which §5.2 perturbs. For text spans (TAT-QA), claims are scored by a
DeBERTa-v3 MNLI/FEVER/ANLI cross-encoder (Laurer et al., 2024 [verify]) with support at
entailment ≥ 0.5, the natural boundary of a three-way softmax rather than a tuned value. The
grounding NLI model is deliberately a different checkpoint from the faithfulness verifier
(§5.1), so that the perturbation audit never tests a model against itself.

We chose operand provenance over entailment for numeric claims after measuring, not assuming,
that NLI scores a correct FinQA answer at ≈ 0.09 entailment: "the contingent rental of 2009 is
19" does not entail "the change was −42.4%", because the missing step is arithmetic.

## 4 Contribution 1: Retrieval Attribution

**Units.** A query is segmented into ordered, non-overlapping units — numbers first, then
named entities (spaCy), then remaining terms, with stopwords kept as their own kind so the
estimators can be checked for wasting mass on them.

**Value functions.** Two are used. *Chunk score*: the retriever's similarity between a query
variant and one retrieved chunk. *Ranking*: rank-biased overlap (Webber et al., 2010) at depth
10 between the ordering induced by a query variant and the original ordering — RankingSHAP's
listwise formulation.

**Estimators.** (i) *Occlusion*: remove each unit alone. (ii) *Shapley*: exact enumeration of
coalitions when a query has ≤ 10 units, otherwise 64 Monte-Carlo permutations (seeded).
(iii) *Surrogate*: a Rank-LIME-style local linear model fitted to 256 sampled coalitions. All
three are training-free; the surrogate is a fresh per-query fit that is discarded.

**Cost.** Every estimator collects all the coalitions it needs before scoring any of them;
variants are embedded once and scored against cached chunk embeddings with a single matrix
multiply, so explaining all top-k chunks costs the same as explaining one. The generator is
never called.

**Evaluation.** With no gold explanation, we report *faithfulness* of the explanation:
comprehensiveness (score drop when the top 20% of units by attribution are removed) and
sufficiency (score retained when only they are kept), each normalised by the full-query score,
and always as a **lift over a random-unit baseline** — removing any 20% of a query lowers the
score somewhat, so a bare comprehensiveness number proves nothing.

## 5 Contribution 2: Auditing the Auditor

### 5.1 Verifiers under audit

RAGAS faithfulness has two stages: *decompose* the (question, answer) pair into atomic
statements with a judge LLM, then *verify* each statement against the context. We keep
decomposition fixed — one pass with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct through RAGAS's own prompts, cached to
disk — so every verifier judges the identical statements, and vary only the verifier:

| verifier | family | parameters | how it is run |
|---|---|---|---|
| `cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-base` | NLI cross-encoder | 184M | statement as hypothesis, joined contexts as premise; supported iff entailment ≥ 0.5 |
| `microsoft/Phi-3.5-mini-instruct` | LLM | 3.8B | RAGAS's unmodified verdict prompt |
| `Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct` | LLM (RAGAS default path) | 7B | RAGAS's unmodified verdict prompt |

The NLI verifier stands in for RAGAS's `FaithfulnesswithHHEM`, which could not be run: Vectara's
HHEM ships custom modelling code that predates `transformers` 5.x and fails to load under the
pinned version that produced every other number (Appendix A). The substitution is exactly the
one RAGAS sanctions; the cross-check in §5.3 measures rather than assumes its consequences.

A caveat stated up front: the 7B LLM verifier is the same model as the generator. §9
discusses why self-preference cannot produce the effects we report, and the 3.8B row is an
independent LLM judge.

### 5.2 Four validity axes

For each verifier *v* and dataset we compute, over the *n* scored questions:

1. **Separation** = mean *v*(correct answers) − mean *v*(incorrect answers), correctness taken
   from the dataset's gold answer (FinQA execution accuracy against `exe_ans` at 1% relative
   tolerance; TAT-QA numeric match with scale, or exact match for spans). A faithfulness
   metric that cannot tell right answers from wrong ones on a numerical benchmark is not
   measuring answer support.
2. **Specificity** (perturbation audit) — three conditions per question: *control* (all
   evidence); *targeted* (remove the sentences the grounding layer identified as supplying the
   answer's operands); *random* (remove an equal number of other sentences, seeded).
   Specificity = (control − targeted) − (control − random). The random arm is essential:
   removing any evidence lowers a score somewhat, so only the *difference* is evidence about the
   metric. Decomposition is invariant under context perturbation, so the sweep costs no LLM
   calls for the NLI verifier and one call per condition for LLM verifiers.
3. **Provenance** = mean *v*(answers whose operands are all provably in the evidence) − mean
   *v*(answers with a missing operand). The reference is deterministic (§3.2): an answer whose
   every consumed figure is in the context is grounded by construction.
4. **Agreement**: statement-level Cohen's κ between the NLI and 7B LLM verdicts on a 50-question
   subset per dataset, aligned by statement text.

Significance for axes 1–3 uses permutation tests with 20,000 resamples (two-sample for
separation and provenance; paired sign-flip for specificity); scores are bounded and bimodal,
so a *t*-test's assumptions do not hold.

### 5.3 Mechanism probe

For every answer we record whether its numeric value appears verbatim (numerically, ignoring
sign and formatting) anywhere in the retrieved evidence — *restated* — or not — *computed*. A
verifier that can only check restatement will score copied figures as faithful and computed
ones as unfaithful regardless of correctness.

### 5.4 The labeled failure-case dataset

Every (question, verifier) pair receives zero or more mechanical labels (threshold 0.5):
**false negative** — correct answer, every operand in the evidence, scored unfaithful;
**false positive** — wrong answer with gold evidence retrieved (or an operand missing), scored
faithful; **insensitive** — targeted removal did not lower a non-zero score; **nonspecific** —
targeted drop ≤ random drop; **unscorable** — no statements. Retrieval failures are excluded
from false positives, since the evidence genuinely does not support the answer there. Each row
carries the question, gold and predicted answers, the expression, the statements and verdicts,
the perturbation scores and the removed sentences.

## 6 Experimental Setup

**Data.** Dev splits of FinQA and TAT-QA; a fixed, seeded subsample of 250 questions per
dataset (nested, so smaller runs are prefixes of larger ones). FinQA's subsample is numeric
(249/250); TAT-QA's has 157 numeric and 93 span questions. TAT-QA table-evidence gold IDs are a
documented best-effort heuristic (the source data has no gold row index) and are used only for
retrieval metrics.

**Scoring.** FinQA: execution accuracy against `exe_ans` — scoring against the rounded display
string instead penalises a model for being *more* precise than the annotation and cost 0.026
in our runs; the ×100 percent relation is gated on the annotation being a percentage. TAT-QA:
numeric match respecting `scale`, exact match and token F1 for spans. Answers that decline
("insufficient evidence") count as wrong throughout.

**Compute and cost.** Generation, statement decomposition, and LLM verification ran on a free
Colab Tesla T4 (`torch 2.11.0+cu128`, `transformers 5.15.0`, `bitsandbytes 0.50.0`); retrieval,
attribution, grounding, NLI verification and all analysis ran on a laptop CPU. No paid API was
used at any stage; a billing guard strips all provider credentials before any RAGAS call and
substitutes a local judge. Per-question checkpoints make every run resumable. The test suite
(189 tests) runs offline.

### 7.1 Baseline (B1)

*Retrieval is not the bottleneck* (Fig. 2, Table 1). With bge-small and per-document scope,
recall@5 is 0.850 on FinQA and 0.843 on TAT-QA, MRR 0.783 / 0.851, and 4.0% / 3.6% of the

*Why the prompt looks the way it does* (Fig. 1). A four-arm ablation (direct answer vs
program-of-thought × 0 vs 3 exemplars) on 50 FinQA questions, replicated at two model sizes,
gives execution accuracy 0.04 / 0.06 / 0.08 / 0.12 at 1.5B and 0.34 / 0.36 / 0.48 / 0.50 at
7B: same ordering at both sizes, program-of-thought dominant (+0.14 at 7B), exemplars +0.02.
Program-of-thought is FinQA's own formulation — the dataset ships a gold program — and it
moves the arithmetic out of the model into an executor, which is where a 7B model loses most
on this task.

### 7.2 Retrieval attribution (Contribution 1)

**Table 2 — Lift in normalised comprehensiveness over a random-unit baseline at the top-20% of
units (`attribution_*_dev_*.json`).**

| value function | n | occlusion | Shapley | surrogate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| FinQA · chunk score | 100 | 0.492 | 0.504 | 0.506 |
| FinQA · ranking (RBO) | 50 | 0.314 | **0.451** | 0.387 |
| TAT-QA · chunk score | 100 | 0.474 | 0.473 | 0.483 |

Every estimator beats random by ≈ 0.47–0.51 on the chunk-score function, where the three are
statistically indistinguishable and agree with each other (Spearman 0.73–0.89). The methods
separate on the *ranking* function, the one that explains an ordering: Shapley's lift (0.451)
is 44% above occlusion's (0.314), with the surrogate between. Where the mass goes is the
second difference (Fig. 5): on FinQA chunk-score attribution Shapley assigns 3.0% of
attribution mass to stopwords against occlusion's 11.3% and the surrogate's 9.0%, and puts
more on numbers (16.2% vs 14.3%). Normalised sufficiency at 20% is small or negative
(FinQA −0.018 for Shapley): keeping only the top units can score *higher* than the full query,
a real property of dense retrieval rather than a defect to tune away.

#### 7.3.1 Separation: does the score know a right answer from a wrong one? (Fig. 8)

Under the NLI verifier, FinQA's *wrong* answers score higher than its correct ones (0.284 vs
0.186; separation −0.098, p = 0.078 — we describe this as "fails to separate, directionally
inverted", since the inversion itself is not significant). Under the 7B LLM verifier the same
answers separate at +0.251 (0.513 vs 0.262, p < 0.001); on TAT-QA +0.242 (0.818 vs 0.576).
The 3.8B LLM verifier reports the **highest mean faithfulness on both datasets** (0.451, 0.725)
and separates on neither (p = 0.30, p = 0.59). A verifier's mean therefore says nothing about
whether it is measuring anything.

#### 7.3.2 Specificity: perturbation sensitivity is necessary, not sufficient (Fig. 10, 11)

Removing the operand-supplying sentence lowers the NLI verifier's score significantly more
than removing an equal number of random sentences on both datasets (FinQA +0.087, p = 0.005;
TAT-QA +0.228, p < 10⁻⁴). **The cheap verifier passes the perturbation audit while failing to
separate correct from wrong answers.** The two are reconciled by the mechanism: the sentence
that supplies the operands is also the sentence that contains the matching numbers, so a
metric tracking lexical overlap and a metric tracking grounding both drop when it is removed.
A perturbation audit cannot tell them apart. Two further observations support this reading.
First, the NLI verifier is *more* specific on FinQA for incorrect answers (0.118) than for
correct ones (0.058) — the wrong direction — whereas the 7B LLM verifier is more specific for
correct answers (0.400 vs 0.167; TAT-QA 0.704 vs 0.586). Second, under the 7B LLM verifier
targeted removal is catastrophic and random removal is not: FinQA 0.373 → 0.034 targeted versus
0.339 random; TAT-QA 0.772 → 0.087 versus 0.743 (specificity +0.305 and +0.656, both p ≤ 10⁻⁴,
on 59/57 paired questions).

*Robustness.* Generation is greedy, the NLI verifier is a deterministic forward pass, and the
targeted arm is chosen by deterministic lookup; the only stochastic component is which random
sentences the control arm removes. Re-running the NLI audit under four seeds (Fig. 11) leaves
control and targeted scores byte-identical and gives specificity +0.070 ± 0.013 on FinQA
(range +0.056 .. +0.087; p < 0.05 under three seeds of four, p = 0.073 under the fourth) and
+0.241 ± 0.012 on TAT-QA (p < 10⁻⁴ under every seed). We quote the seed mean, not the most
favourable seed.

#### 7.3.3 Provenance: agreement with a deterministic reference

Against operand provenance — a reference with no model in it — the NLI verifier's separation
between grounded and ungrounded answers is −0.023 (FinQA) and +0.003 (TAT-QA): none. The LLM
verifiers are positive but only Phi-3.5 on TAT-QA reaches significance (+0.427, p = 0.015);
the reference is coarse — of scored answers with numeric claims, 82% (FinQA, 166/203) and
88% (TAT-QA, 74/84) are fully grounded, leaving few ungrounded answers to compare against —
so this axis has less power than the others, and we report it as corroboration rather than as
a headline.

#### 7.3.4 Inter-verifier agreement and mechanism (Fig. 14, 9)

On 50 questions per dataset, statement-level agreement between the NLI and 7B LLM verdicts is
0.531 (FinQA, 49 statements) and 0.494 (TAT-QA, 77) — Cohen's κ **−0.147** and **+0.157**, at or
below chance for two verifiers a library presents as interchangeable. The disagreement is
one-sided on TAT-QA: the LLM verifier supports 39 statements the NLI verifier rejects, and the
NLI verifier supports none the LLM rejects.

The mechanism is restatement. Faithfulness by whether the answer's value appears in the
evidence (`failure_cases_*_dev.jsonl`; FinQA n = 35 restated / 200 computed, TAT-QA 112 / 78):

| verifier | FinQA restated | FinQA computed | TAT-QA restated | TAT-QA computed |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| NLI cross-encoder | 0.476 | 0.195 | 0.502 | 0.154 |
| LLM Phi-3.5-mini | 0.771 | 0.395 | 0.888 | 0.449 |
| LLM Qwen2.5-7B | 0.514 | 0.360 | 0.832 | 0.474 |

Every verifier rewards restatement (the gaps for the NLI verifier are p < 0.001 on both
datasets), but the NLI verifier scores a *computed* FinQA answer at 0.195 on average — a
correct answer such as `3280 / 19686 × 100 = 16.66%` with both operands in the evidence is
scored 0.0, because "revenue from agriculture was 3,280" does not *entail* "16.66% of
revenue". That step is arithmetic, which no entailment model performs.

nonspecific on FinQA) against the NLI verifier's 27 and 3.

### 7.4 The composed system: B1 / B2 / B3 and selective accuracy (Fig. 12)

All three baselines share the frozen generator, so B3 is not a different answerer; it is a
different *gate*. Each baseline exposes a "verified" flag, and we ask what a user who trusts
only flagged answers ends up holding.

**Table 5 — Selective accuracy at threshold 0.5 (`b3_comparison.json`). Coverage = share of
answers flagged; correct = 113/250 (FinQA), 119/250 (TAT-QA).**

| flag | FinQA cov. | FinQA acc. verified | TAT-QA cov. | TAT-QA acc. verified |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| B1 — trust everything | 1.000 | 0.452 | 1.000 | 0.476 |
| B2 — RAGAS faithful ≥ 0.5, NLI verifier | 0.220 | **0.382** | 0.356 | 0.607 |
| B2 — RAGAS faithful ≥ 0.5, Phi-3.5 | 0.424 | 0.519 | 0.668 | 0.521 |
| B2 — RAGAS faithful ≥ 0.5, Qwen-7B | 0.360 | 0.644 | 0.648 | 0.599 |
| B3 — operand provenance only | 0.692 | 0.538 | 0.668 | 0.575 |
| B3 — provenance ∧ faithful (NLI) | 0.152 | 0.474 | 0.268 | 0.657 |
| B3 — provenance ∧ faithful (Phi-3.5) | 0.312 | 0.577 | 0.508 | 0.575 |
| **B3 — provenance ∧ faithful (Qwen-7B)** | 0.268 | **0.716** | 0.500 | **0.640** |

On FinQA the cheap verifier's flag is *worse than no flag*: answers it marks faithful are
correct 38% of the time against 45% for the whole set — the inverted separation of §7.3.1 as a
user-facing consequence. The deterministic provenance gate alone lifts precision to 0.54 at 69%
coverage; adding the validated 7B verifier lifts it to **0.72** at 27% coverage (48 of the 113
correct answers retained). On TAT-QA the same gate reaches 0.64 at 50% coverage. The
grounding-only row is worth noting for deployment: it needs no LLM call at all and already
raises trusted-answer accuracy by 9–10 points on both datasets.

Grounding itself (Fig. 6): groundedness 0.757 (FinQA) / 0.664 (TAT-QA), grounding to the gold
evidence for 68.8% / 58.0% of answers, contradiction 8.8% / 18.0%; it is diagnostic of outcome
(FinQA groundedness 0.88 for correct answers, 0.75 for generation failures, 0.22 for retrieval
failures).

## 8 Discussion

**Verifier capacity, not verifier type.** The natural summary of Table 3 is not "LLM good,
NLI bad". The 3.8B LLM verifier fails the separation axis as completely as the NLI
cross-encoder while reporting the highest mean of any verifier; the 7B LLM passes every axis.
What the failing verifiers share is an inability to perform — or to recognise the need for —
the arithmetic that links evidence to answer on this domain. A higher reported faithfulness
score is not a better verifier; it may be a more credulous one.

**A domain-specific counterexample, not a general indictment.** RAGBench, ARES and RAGAS's own
HHEM variant are right that cheap verifiers suffice on the general-domain data they were
evaluated on, where support is textual. Our claim is narrower and, we think, more useful: on
numerical QA the substitution inverts the metric, the mechanism is identifiable, and the
inversion is invisible to the one check practitioners usually run — a perturbation test. Any
faithfulness number reported for financial RAG should therefore name its verifier, and the
metric should be reported as a table across verifiers rather than as a scalar.

**Perturbation sensitivity is necessary but not sufficient.** This is the methodological point
we would most like readers to take away. A verifier can respond to the right evidence for the
wrong reason, and the audit that establishes validity needs a second, independent axis —
correctness or a deterministic reference — that perturbation alone cannot supply.

**What the explanation layer buys.** Attribution and grounding are supporting machinery here,
but they are what make the audit possible: grounding names the sentence to remove, and it
provides the deterministic reference. The same machinery, composed into a gate, is what turns
a 0.45-accurate system into one whose trusted answers are 0.72 accurate.

---

## 9 Limitations

- **Self-grading.** The best-performing verifier is the generator itself (Qwen2.5-7B). Three
  facts argue that self-preference cannot manufacture the reported effects: separation is
  measured against gold-answer correctness and provenance against a deterministic reference,
  neither of which the judge can influence; self-preference would inflate the *mean*, yet the
  independent 3.8B LLM reports a higher mean than Qwen with no separation; and all verifiers
  judge identical cached statements. An independent 7B judge run was attempted and is reported
  in the released results if it completed; if absent, this confound stands as stated.
- **Sample size.** 250 questions per dataset, dev splits only; the LLM-verifier perturbation
  audit covers 59 / 57 paired questions; attribution 100 (chunk score) and 50 (ranking); the
  cross-check 50. Effects that matter are large and significant at these sizes, but the
  provenance axis is under-powered.
- **HHEM not run.** RAGAS's actual `FaithfulnesswithHHEM` could not be loaded under the pinned
  `transformers` version; a standard NLI cross-encoder stands in. Three NLI checkpoints were
  swept during development and reported mean faithfulness 0.163 / 0.237 / 0.383 on identical
  FinQA data; only the middle one is in the released tables.
- **One generator.** Verifier behaviour could depend on the generator's answer style; the
  generator is a fixed instrument here, and its 0.45 execution accuracy is far below published
  FinQA systems. Improving answer accuracy is explicitly not this paper's claim.
- **FinQA specificity under the NLI verifier** is significant under three of four seeds; we
  report the seed mean.
- **No human faithfulness labels.** Every axis is automatic by design; a human spot-check of
  verdicts would strengthen the mechanism claim.
- **TAT-QA table gold IDs** are heuristic; they affect retrieval metrics only.

---

## 10 Conclusion

We audited the auditor. Holding a financial RAG system fixed and varying only the verifier
inside RAGAS's faithfulness metric, we found that the cheap verifier the field recommends
inverts on numerical QA — scoring wrong answers as more faithful than right ones, agreeing with
the LLM verifier at chance, and collapsing to lexical restatement because it cannot do
arithmetic — while still passing the perturbation test practitioners rely on. A 7B LLM verifier
passes every axis; a 3.8B one fails, with the highest mean of all. We release the four-axis
protocol, a labeled failure-case dataset, a training-free attribution layer, and a system
whose verification gate raises the accuracy of trusted answers from 0.45 to 0.72, all at zero
monetary cost on open weights.
